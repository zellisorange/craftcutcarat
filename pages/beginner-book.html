About this book

Designed collaboratively by Andrew Ian Brown and Mark Oros of Hashnu Stones & Gems, this book walks beginning faceters through twelve progressively challenging designs — from a simple nonagon brilliant to a stunning frosted hexagon. Each design uses optimized angles for multiple RI ranges so you always get the best performance from whatever material you're cutting.

📐
Meet-point faceting
Every design follows meet-point principles with detailed sequencing so you always know which facet to cut next and what it should meet.
🔢
Multiple RI sets
Designs include A, B, and sometimes C angle optimizations. Match your stone's refractive index to the table below and use the right set.
⬆
Progressive difficulty
Start with the Nonagon Brilliant (31 facets) and work up to the Frost Star Hex (79 facets). Each design introduces a new skill.
✨
Odd symmetry magic
Many designs use 3-fold, 5-fold, or 7-fold odd symmetry to produce exceptional scintillation from a surprisingly small number of facets.

The twelve designs

Cut them in order. Repeat a design before moving on if needed. Each shape introduces something new.

Design 01 · Nonagon
Brilliant 93
AIB251
31 facets 3-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.20
Your starting point. A simple round design using 3-fold odd symmetry on a 96 index. Focus on forming a perfect final center point (FCP) — the entire stone is built from it.
Design 02 · Pentagon
Five Steps
AIB252
21 facets 1-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.20
Introduction to a stepped crown. Uses imperfect 5-fold symmetry on the 96 index. Get your transfer spot-on — even crown steps depend on it.
Design 03 · Trillion
Trioptic Blast
AIB253
37 facets 3-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.40
Triangular cut with cut corners for easy mounting. Outstanding performance from 3-fold symmetry. Take care with the fine P2 facets that set the girdle outline.
Design 04 · Marquise
Easy Cut Marquise
AIB254
33 facets 2-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.00
Named after the lips of Madame de Pompadour. Transfer accuracy is critical on this long thin stone. The table will challenge beginners — take your time.
Design 05 · Square
Little Princess
AIB255
49 facets 4-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.20
Simplified princess cut. Excellent performance even at lower RI. Watch tiers C2 and C3 carefully — same index, ~5° difference. Don't rush them.
Design 06 · Pear
Seven Star Pear
AIB256
36 facets 1-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.40
Imperfect 7-fold symmetry on P1 creates sparkling scintillation. Two barion-like tiers level the girdle. Table meet points may need gentle adjustment after polishing.
Design 07 · Decagon
DecaCheck Two
AIB257
36 facets 1-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.20
Your first checker crown — easier than it looks with only a small number of crown facets. Half the G1 facets don't form a level girdle until P2 is cut.
Design 08 · Shield/Heart
Shield Of The Heart
AIB258
31 facets 1-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.20
3-fold symmetry on P1 creates outstanding scintillation. Introduces barion facets (P3, P4) — half-moon facets first used by Basil Watermeyer in 1971.
Design 09 · Oval
Beginner Oval 1.40
AIB259
47 facets 2-fold symmetry RI 1.54–1.90
More tiers than previous designs — takes longer but is still straightforward. Follow the pavilion sequence carefully to set the girdle outline correctly.
Design 10 · Round
Six Star Brilliant
AIB260
67 facets 3-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.40
An exceptional round brilliant combining 6-fold and 3-fold symmetry on an 18-sided round. Exceptional performance especially in quartz, topaz, and sapphire.
Design 11 · Rectangle
150 Emerald
AIB261
37 facets 2-fold symmetry RI 1.54–1.90
Introduces a preform to set the girdle outline — a great learning step. The preform tiers are later cut away by pavilion facets. Measure carefully before you begin pavilion cutting.
Design 12 · Hexagon
Frost Star Hex
AIB262
79 facets 6-fold symmetry RI 1.54–2.30 ★ Frosting
The grand finale. Introduces facet-frosting using a worn 800-grit bronze sintered lap. P3, P4, and P5 cut VERY fast — go slowly and check with a hand lens. Practice on a test stone first.

Mark's guide to your first twelve gemstones

Mark Oros of Hashnu Stones & Gems shares the hard-won wisdom he gives every student before they head home to cut alone.

1
Monitor your progress. Every gemstone needs to be better than the last. As long as you're moving forward, you're on the right path.
2
Practice, practice, practice. It takes a minimum of seven to twelve gemstones to start working through the basics. Tiffany, Voulkos, and O'Keeffe didn't produce masterpieces on their first tries either.
3
Start with 10 mm rough. Light smoky quartz is ideal — you can see the facets clearly, it's inexpensive, and it won't disappear if you make a mistake. Cover the stone with pencil between laps to see what you've cut.
4
Advice comes from many sources — filter it. Beware of anyone insisting their way is the only way. Balance reading and watching with actually cutting. The only way to learn to facet is to facet.
5
Keep a journal. Track your failures and successes. Each design in this book has a notes page — use it. Your Galaxy Gems Journal is perfect for this.
6
Start simple, progress deliberately. Baby steps are fine. Don't overstep your progress by leaping ahead. Read "Learn to Facet the Right Way" by Jeff Graham.
7
Trust your machine. If you've cut one to three stones and are blaming the machine — it's most likely not the machine. Put the time in first. Buy a good machine and trust it to do its job while you learn yours.
8
Don't skip laps. All faceting does is replace larger scratches with smaller ones up to the polished state. Each lap takes you along a gradual path. Skipping laps limits precision and makes polishing a chore.
9
Watch what each lap is doing. Make sure laps are clean and not contaminated. It's very common to think scratches came from the previous lap when the current lap simply needs to be cleaned.
10
Build your foundation carefully. The girdle, pavilion girdle facets, and crown girdle facets are the foundation. Go around these tiers two or three times on each lap to set everything correctly before moving on.
11
The extra pass saves time. It takes a little longer to go around the tiers again, but it saves you time putting in all the following facets correctly.
12
Take care of your equipment. Clean laps, maintained machine, tidy workspace. They will take care of you in return.
13
Ask for help. If a problem keeps repeating, stop and ask the community. The only unanswered question is the one not asked. When in doubt, ask about.
14
Don't rush dopping. Ensure significant surface-area contact. Clean and oil-free surfaces. Let the glue set overnight. Rushing this step costs you later.
15
Buy rough carefully. Only buy from dealers with a return policy. Inspect rough on receipt. If a deal seems too good to be true from an unknown seller — it probably is.
16
Set realistic expectations. Come back with a clear head if frustration hits. Your first twelve gemstones are practice. Keep the queue moving while you build your craft.

The four P's of buying facet rough

Mark Oros's framework for thinking about your rough gemstone purchases — from practice stones to passion pieces.

P
Practice
The rough you use to learn how to facet. Buy a variety of materials and sizes that match these 12 designs. Light smoky quartz is ideal — affordable and easy to see.
P
Portfolio
Rough you facet to show clients. You need examples of your work to demonstrate your style and capabilities. These stones are for sale, but keep a reasonable portfolio until established.
P
Purchase
Rough you cut to fill specific orders from clients. Many prefer to cut on request and use Portfolio stones for immediate sales rather than maintaining a large finished inventory.
P
Passion
The rough that needs no serious justification. These are the reward for successful selling — the stones you love, in species that excite you. Buy rare material when it appears.
